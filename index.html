Money is too effective in campaigns.

Campaign capital converts into narrative dominance — not because voters are stupid, but because low civics literacy, media fragmentation and partisan affinity make emotional, high-frequency advertising beat non-partisan information every single time.

01 — The situation

This isn't a puzzle. It's a knot.

A bounded problem has a solution waiting at the end of enough analysis. This one doesn't. Four forces hold it in place, and each one re-arms the others.

Every intervention so far has been metabolised by the system it was aimed at.

  • Legal & structural

    Citizens United anchors campaign spending in constitutional First Amendment protection. Reform keeps hitting the same wall.

  • Cognitive & social

    Voters aren't passive blank slates. They actively seek partisan narratives that confirm their identity — motivated reasoning, not gullibility.

  • Systemic feedback

    When fact-checkers intervene, partisan actors brand the fact-checkers as biased. The intervention is neutralised and civic distrust deepens.

  • Economic incentive

    Platform algorithms and media companies profit directly from high-conflict political ad spend. Nobody in the loop is paid to close it.

02 — How we know

No client handed us this.
The evidence did.

Seven extracts from a civics survey climbed the ladder into one theme. Here's the whole trail — every claim below is traceable to a source that ships in this repo.

Theme · tier 3

$ is king

Money funds more partisan sources, and partisan sources are more powerful than non-partisan ones for election information.

Pattern · tier 2

More $ = more wins

The more money groups put into campaigns, the more elections they win.

  • Campaign Finance — reform efforts consistently hit roadblocks like the Citizens United decision.
  • $ in politics — entrenched actors (AIPAC), ideological. New actors: crypto, AI companies.
  • District competitiveness — financial influence and gerrymandering have reduced the competitiveness of congressional districts.
Pattern · tier 2

People seek bad sources

People are more persuaded by partisan messaging than by neutral information — so objective fact-checkers hold a sliver of the influence that vested interests do.

  • Civic distrust & disinformation — distrust in voting, thin civics literacy and internet-era disinformation let political entities manipulate voters' worst instincts.
  • Voter literacy — why do ads have such a strong effect? Why does money in elections work?
  • Media misinformation — misleading media and digital influencing are damaging civic engagement.
  • Accountability for public lies — telling the truth, and holding liars accountable, is named as the missing piece of civic life.

Blind spot — boundary & flux

We have no data on where money stops working — presidential versus municipal, statewide versus ballot initiative — and none on how generative AI and micro-targeting are changing the unit cost of persuasion.

Blind spot — counterfactuals

We have zero evidence on campaigns where the outspent candidate won. Without it we're assuming money's power is total rather than conditional. It's probably conditional.

A pod that closes those three turns a strong opinion into a defensible frame.

Method
Expert qualitative interviews — 3–5 campaign consultants, across parties

Method

→ Settles whether AI democratises campaign outreach or hands legacy donors a bigger lever.

06 — The opposition

Three groups are paid for this to stay exactly as it is.

Not a conspiracy — an incentive structure. Which is worse, because it needs nobody to coordinate it.

  • Ad-tech & broadcast media

    Billions in guaranteed, high-margin revenue every election cycle — regardless of who wins.

  • Incumbents & interest groups

    High financial entry barriers insulate them from grassroots challengers. The cost is the moat.

  • Campaign agencies

    Paid 10–15% of total media buy. A direct, structural incentive to tell clients to raise more and spend more.

What could break our framing

The “voter as passive object” fallacy. Our cards currently assume voters are dupes who are easily persuaded by ads. That ignores identity-driven choice — voters often want low-veracity partisan messaging because it validates their social group. If that's the stronger explanation, the whole frame moves.

Why we're saying that out loud

We scored our own paradox 0 out of 3 on sharpness. It doesn't yet prove both legs of the contradiction, and it hasn't named an owner who'd hold it. A pod that can't state its own weakness isn't worth six weeks of your attention.
